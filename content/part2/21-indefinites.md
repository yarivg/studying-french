# Someone, something, anything

English has a tidy grid: *some-* and *any-* and *no-* crossed with *-one*, *-thing*, *-where*.
French has the same grid, but each cell is its own word, and the negative half drags a **ne**
into the sentence with it. Chapter 16 listed those negatives; this chapter puts them back
beside their positive partners, and adds the two things nobody teaches: the **de** that appears
before an adjective, and the whole *n'importe* family.

## The grid

| | positive | negative |
|---|---|---|
| person | **quelqu'un** someone | **personne** nobody |
| thing | **quelque chose** something | **rien** nothing |
| place | **quelque part** somewhere | **nulle part** nowhere |
| one of a set | **quelques-uns / quelques-unes** some, a few | **aucun / aucune** not one |

::: examples
Quelqu'un a téléphoné. || Someone called.
J'ai vu quelque chose de bizarre. || I saw something odd.
Mes clés sont quelque part ici. || My keys are somewhere here.
J'ai lu quelques-uns de ces livres. || I've read some of these books.
:::

::: note quelqu'un never changes
*Quelqu'un* is invariable and always masculine singular for agreement, whoever it refers to:
*quelqu'un est venu*, even if that someone was your sister. The feminine *quelqu'une* exists
only in the plural *quelques-unes*.
:::

# The negatives need their ne

Every negative in the right-hand column is one half of a negation. The other half is **ne**
in front of the verb, exactly as in Chapter 16. Leaving it out is the single most common
mistake with these words.

::: examples
Je ne vois personne. || I see nobody.
Il n'y a rien dans le frigo. || There's nothing in the fridge.
Je ne vais nulle part ce week-end. || I'm going nowhere this weekend.
Je n'ai aucune idée. || I have no idea.
:::

::: warning Never *je vois personne* in writing
Spoken French drops the *ne* constantly: *y'a rien*, *j'vais nulle part*. Understand it, write
the *ne*. The same rule as Chapter 16, and it applies to every word in this chapter.
:::

## When the negative is the subject

*Personne*, *rien* and *aucun* can be the subject of the verb. Then they come first, and **ne**
still sits in front of the verb, with nothing after it.

::: examples
Personne n'est venu. || Nobody came.
Rien n'a changé. || Nothing has changed.
Aucun train ne part avant huit heures. || No train leaves before eight.
Personne ne le sait. || Nobody knows.
:::

::: rule Two positions, one ne
Object: *je ne connais **personne***, the word comes after the verb.
Subject: ***Personne** ne me connaît*, the word comes before it.
Either way *ne* touches the verb. If you have written *personne* or *rien* and there is no *ne*
anywhere, the sentence is not finished.
:::

::: note Where they sit in the passé composé
The Chapter 16 split holds here: *rien* is light and goes before the participle, *personne*
and *nulle part* are heavy and go after it. *Je n'ai **rien** vu*, but *je n'ai vu **personne***
and *je ne suis allé **nulle part***.
:::

# The de before an adjective

This is the rule learners never meet and always get wrong. When you attach an adjective to one
of these words, you need **de** between them.

::: examples
quelque chose **de** beau || something beautiful
quelque chose **d'**important || something important
rien **de** grave || nothing serious
rien **de** nouveau || nothing new
quelqu'un **d'**intéressant || someone interesting
personne **d'**autre || nobody else
quelque part **d'**agréable || somewhere pleasant
Quoi **de** neuf ? || What's new?
:::

::: rule The adjective is always masculine singular
Whatever the word actually refers to, the adjective after *de* takes its bare masculine
singular form. *J'ai rencontré quelqu'un d'**intelligent*** is what you say about a woman too.
*Quelques-unes* is the exception, because it is a real plural: *quelques-unes sont **cassées***.
:::

::: warning Not *quelque chose beau*, not *quelque chose de belle*
Two errors, both frequent. Dropping the *de* is the English word order surviving; making the
adjective agree is the ordinary adjective rule leaking in where it does not belong.
:::

::: examples
Ce n'est rien de grave. || It's nothing serious.
Il n'y a personne d'autre ici. || There's nobody else here.
J'ai besoin de quelqu'un de sérieux. || I need someone reliable.
:::

::: tip The same de before an infinitive
*Rien à faire* and *quelque chose à manger* take **à**, not *de*, when what follows is a verb:
*j'ai quelque chose **à** te dire*, *il n'y a rien **à** faire*. Adjective takes *de*,
infinitive takes *à*.
:::

# N'importe

*N'importe* plus a question word means *any at all, it does not matter which*. Literally
"it does not matter who / what / where".

| | |
|---|---|
| **n'importe qui** | anyone at all |
| **n'importe quoi** | anything at all |
| **n'importe où** | anywhere at all |
| **n'importe quand** | any time at all |
| **n'importe comment** | any old how |
| **n'importe lequel / laquelle / lesquels / lesquelles** | any one of them |
| **n'importe quel / quelle + noun** | any … at all |

::: examples
N'importe qui peut le faire. || Anyone can do it.
Prends n'importe lequel. || Take any of them.
On peut se voir n'importe quand. || We can meet any time.
Appelle-moi à n'importe quelle heure. || Call me at any hour.
Il mange n'importe quoi. || He eats anything.
:::

::: rule quel takes a noun, lequel replaces one
*N'importe **quel** livre*: the noun is there, so *quel* agrees with it: *quelle robe*,
*quels jours*. *N'importe **lequel***: the noun is gone, so the pronoun carries its gender
and number alone. Same division as the ordinary *quel* / *lequel* pair.
:::

## The dismissive reading

Every one of these has a second, negative use, and it is far commoner in speech than the
neutral one. Said of a person or of what they do, *n'importe quoi* means nonsense, and
*n'importe comment* means carelessly.

::: examples
Il dit n'importe quoi. || He's talking rubbish.
Tu racontes n'importe quoi ! || You're talking nonsense!
C'est n'importe quoi, ce film. || That film is garbage.
Il a fait ça n'importe comment. || He did it any old how, badly.
Ne fais pas n'importe quoi. || Don't do anything stupid.
:::

::: note Which reading is it?
Context, and the verb. *Prends n'importe lequel* is an offer, so it is neutral. *Dire*,
*raconter* and *faire* with *n'importe quoi* are almost always the dismissive one. The single
most useful phrase here is *c'est n'importe quoi*, which you will hear daily.
:::

# Chaque, chacun, quelconque

**Chaque** is a determiner and needs its noun. **Chacun / chacune** is the pronoun that stands
alone. Both are singular, always.

::: examples
Chaque étudiant a un livre. || Each student has a book.
Chacun a son livre. || Each one has their book.
Chacune de ces photos est ancienne. || Each of these photos is old.
Ils coûtent dix euros chacun. || They cost ten euros each.
:::

::: warning *Chaque* never stands alone
*Chaque a un livre* is wrong: say *chacun a un livre*. And there is no *chaques*: the word has
no plural, because each is counted one at a time.
:::

**Quelconque** goes after the noun and means *some … or other*, often with a shrug:
*pour une raison quelconque* (for some reason or other). On its own as an adjective it means
*ordinary, unremarkable*: *un film quelconque* is a forgettable film.

# Tout le monde

*Tout le monde* means *everybody*, and it is grammatically singular. The verb is singular, and
so is anything that agrees with it.

::: examples
Tout le monde est là. || Everybody is here.
Tout le monde a compris. || Everybody understood.
Tout le monde veut partir. || Everybody wants to leave.
:::

::: warning Not *tout le monde sont*
The English plural is the trap. *Le monde* is the head, and it is singular, so the verb is
singular too. The same goes for *chacun*: *chacun **fait** ce qu'il veut*, never *font*.
:::

For *tout* itself as a determiner and a pronoun - *tout le temps*, *tous les jours*,
*j'ai tout mangé* - see Chapter 11 of Part I.

# Pairs that get confused

::: rule quelque chose or quelque part?
*Chose* is a thing, *part* is a place. *J'ai oublié quelque **chose*** = I forgot something.
*Je l'ai vu quelque **part*** = I saw it somewhere. Their negatives split the same way:
*rien* for the thing, *nulle part* for the place.
:::

::: rule personne the pronoun or une personne the noun?
With no article, *personne* is the negative pronoun and needs *ne*: *je ne vois **personne***.
With an article, *une personne* is an ordinary feminine noun meaning *a person*:
*une personne m'attend*. Note the gender stays feminine even for a man:
*cette personne est arrivée*.
:::

::: rule rien or aucun?
*Rien* is a pronoun and replaces a whole thing: *je n'ai **rien** acheté*. *Aucun* attaches to
a noun or points back at one, and it agrees: *je n'ai acheté **aucun** livre*,
*je n'ai lu **aucune** de ces pages*. If there is a noun in the sentence, you want *aucun*.
:::
