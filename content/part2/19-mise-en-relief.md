# Putting the emphasis somewhere

English emphasises with the voice. *I* didn't say that, *I* didn't say **that**, *I didn't say that*: three
different sentences, same eight letters on the page, and only the stress tells them apart. French will
not do this. The voice stays flat across the sentence, so when a French speaker wants one word to carry
the weight, the sentence is rebuilt around it.

That rebuilding is called **mise en relief**, putting something into relief. There are four machines,
and between them they cover almost everything you will hear.

::: note Why not just move the word?
Because French word order is load-bearing. *Pierre voit Marie* and *Marie voit Pierre* are not the same
sentence with different emphasis: they are two different events. English can shunt a word to the front
and rely on the verb ending or on stress to keep the roles straight. French cannot, so emphasis has to be
a construction rather than a rearrangement.
:::

# Machine 1: c'est ... qui, for the subject

Take the subject out of the sentence, wrap it in *c'est ... qui*, and leave the rest behind.

**C'est + le sujet + qui + verbe.**

::: examples
Pierre a cassé le vase. → C'est Pierre qui a cassé le vase. || Pierre is the one who broke the vase.
Ma sœur conduit. → C'est ma sœur qui conduit. || it's my sister who's driving.
:::

With a pronoun, the stressed form goes in the slot: *moi, toi, lui, elle, nous, vous, eux, elles*.
Never *je*, *tu*, *il*.

::: examples
C'est moi qui ai fait ça. || I'm the one who did that.
C'est toi qui as raison. || you're the one who's right.
C'est nous qui sommes en retard. || we're the ones who are late.
:::

::: rule The verb agrees with the stressed pronoun, not with c'est
This is the whole trap. *Qui* passes the person straight through to the verb, and *c'est* has nothing to
do with it.

*C'est **moi qui fais** la cuisine.* - not *qui fait*.
*C'est **toi qui as** raison.* - not *qui a*.
*C'est **nous qui sommes** en retard.* - not *qui est*.
*Ce sont **eux qui ont** décidé.* - not *qui a*.

Read it as two halves: *c'est moi* is one clause, *qui fais la cuisine* is another, and the second one
takes its person from the pronoun sitting in front of it.
:::

::: warning The third person is where the ear misleads you
*C'est moi qui **suis** le premier* sounds odd to an English ear and is nevertheless correct. Careless
speech does produce *c'est moi qui est*, and it is simply wrong.

The reason the mistake survives is that in the third person nothing gives it away: *c'est lui qui est*
is right, and *c'est* and *qui est* agree with each other by accident. Test yourself on *moi*, *toi* and
*nous*, where the verb has to change shape.
:::

## Ce sont, and the c'est that replaces it

Before a plural noun or *eux / elles*, the careful form is **ce sont**.

::: examples
Ce sont mes parents qui décident. || my parents are the ones who decide.
Ce sont eux qui ont commencé. || they're the ones who started it.
:::

::: note What people actually say
In speech, *c'est* swallows *ce sont* almost everywhere: *c'est eux qui ont commencé*, *c'est mes parents
qui décident*. Nobody will blink. In writing, and in an exam, use *ce sont*.

*Ce sont* is only for the third person plural. *C'est nous*, *c'est vous*: never *ce sommes*, never
*ce sont vous*.
:::

# Machine 2: c'est ... que, for everything else

If what you are stressing is not the subject, the connector is **que**. The slot takes an object, a time,
a place, a manner, a reason: anything at all.

::: examples
Je veux ce livre. → C'est ce livre que je veux. || this book is the one I want.
Je pars demain. → C'est demain que je pars. || it's tomorrow that I leave.
Il habite à Paris. → C'est à Paris qu'il habite. || Paris is where he lives.
Je l'ai fait pour toi. → C'est pour toi que je l'ai fait. || it was for you that I did it.
Je viens le lundi. → C'est le lundi que je viens. || Monday is when I come.
:::

::: rule qui for the subject, que for the rest
Nothing else decides it. Ask what the highlighted word was doing in the plain sentence.

*C'est Pierre **qui** a cassé le vase.* - Pierre was the subject, so *qui*.
*C'est le vase **que** Pierre a cassé.* - the vase was the object, so *que*.

The *qui* here is not "who" and the *que* is not "that": they are the same relative pronouns as in
Chapter 9, choosing by grammatical role, not by whether the thing is a person.
:::

::: warning The preposition travels with the word
When you highlight something that was introduced by a preposition, the preposition goes into the slot
with it, not out on its own.

*C'est **à Paris** qu'il habite.* - not *c'est Paris qu'il habite à*.
*C'est **de toi** que je parle.* - not *c'est toi que je parle de*.

French never strands a preposition at the end. That is an English habit and it has no French equivalent.
:::

::: tip Elision
*Que* elides before a vowel, *qui* never does.
*C'est à Paris **qu'il** habite*, *c'est demain **qu'elle** part* - but *c'est lui **qui** a payé*, and
never *qu'a payé*.
:::

# Machine 3: ce qui / ce que / ce dont ... c'est

The split sentence. It puts the whole of one idea on the left, a comma in the middle, and the thing being
stressed on the right, after *c'est*.

**Ce qui / Ce que / Ce dont + clause, c'est + la chose.**

::: examples
Ce qui m'intéresse, c'est la musique. || what interests me is music.
Ce qui compte, c'est l'intention. || what counts is the intention.
Ce que je veux, c'est partir. || what I want is to leave.
Ce dont j'ai besoin, c'est d'un café. || what I need is a coffee.
:::

Chapter 5 of Part III teaches *ce qui / ce que / ce dont* as relative pronouns, the ones that stand in for
a whole idea rather than a named noun. That is the same machinery. Here it is being used for emphasis:
the left half names the topic, the right half delivers the point.

::: rule Choose by what the verb on the left needs
*ce **qui*** - the thing is the **subject** of that verb: *ce qui **me plaît***, *ce qui **est arrivé***.
*ce **que*** - the thing is its **object**: *ce **que** je veux*, *ce **que** tu as dit*.
*ce **dont*** - the verb takes **de**: *avoir besoin **de***, *parler **de***, *avoir envie **de***.

Same test as any relative: look for the hole in the clause and see what shape it is.
:::

::: warning ce dont keeps its de on the other side too
This is the detail that gets missed. If the verb needed *de*, the *de* reappears after *c'est*.

*Ce dont j'ai besoin, c'est **d'**un café.*
*Ce dont j'ai envie, c'est **d'**un week-end au calme.*

Not *c'est un café*. The verb is still *avoir besoin de*, and both halves have to honour it.
:::

# Machine 4: dislocation, the spoken one

The most frequent pattern of all, and the one that looks wrong to an English eye. Pull a word out to the
front or the back, and leave a pronoun behind doing its job in the sentence.

::: examples
Moi, je pense que c'est une erreur. || me, I think it's a mistake.
Lui, il ne sait rien. || him, he doesn't know a thing.
Le film, je l'ai vu hier. || the film, I saw it yesterday.
Je l'ai vu hier, le film. || I saw it yesterday, the film.
Ce livre, je n'en ai jamais entendu parler. || that book, I've never heard of it.
:::

::: rule The pronoun is duplicated on purpose
*Moi, je pense* has two subjects on paper and one in meaning. *Le film, je l'ai vu* names the film twice.
This is not sloppy speech, it is not redundancy to be corrected, and it is everywhere: conversation,
journalism, dialogue in novels.

The rule is simply that the sentence must still be complete without the dislocated piece. *Moi, je pense*
works because *je pense* stands on its own. *Moi pense* does not exist.
:::

::: tip Front or back?
Front sets the topic up before you comment on it: *le film, je l'ai vu*. Back adds it as an afterthought,
often to clear up who or what you meant: *je l'ai vu, le film*. Both are correct, and the front position
is the more common of the two.
:::

::: warning Stressed pronoun at the front, never the subject form
*Moi, je pense.* - not *je, je pense*. The dislocated pronoun is always the stressed form
(*moi, toi, lui, elle, nous, vous, eux, elles*), and the ordinary subject pronoun stays in its place in
front of the verb.

*Nous* and *vous* look the same in both slots, which is why they cause no trouble and teach nothing:
test yourself on *moi*, *lui* and *eux*.
:::

# Choosing, in one table

| What you are stressing | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| the subject | *c'est ... qui* | *C'est moi qui ai payé.* |
| a plural subject, written | *ce sont ... qui* | *Ce sont eux qui ont payé.* |
| an object, a time, a place | *c'est ... que* | *C'est demain que je pars.* |
| a whole idea, subject side | *ce qui ..., c'est* | *Ce qui compte, c'est l'intention.* |
| a whole idea, object side | *ce que ..., c'est* | *Ce que je veux, c'est partir.* |
| a whole idea, with de | *ce dont ..., c'est de* | *Ce dont j'ai besoin, c'est d'un café.* |
| a person or thing, in speech | dislocation | *Moi, je pense que non.* |

C'est toi qui (avoir) raison. || C'est toi qui as raison.
C'est nous qui (être) en retard. || C'est nous qui sommes en retard.

C'est vous qui (partir) demain. || C'est vous qui partez demain.
:::

::: exercise qui or que?
C'est Pierre ___ a cassé le vase. || qui - Pierre was the subject
C'est le vase ___ Pierre a cassé. || que - the vase was the object
C'est demain ___ je pars. || que - a time, not a subject
C'est ma sœur ___ conduit. || qui - she is doing the driving
C'est à Paris ___ il habite. || qu' - qu'il, que elides before a vowel
:::

::: exercise ce qui, ce que or ce dont?
___ m'intéresse, c'est la musique. || Ce qui - it is the subject of intéresse
___ je veux, c'est partir. || Ce que - it is the object of veux
___ j'ai besoin, c'est d'un café. || Ce dont - avoir besoin de
___ compte, c'est l'intention. || Ce qui
___ il parle, c'est de son enfance. || Ce dont - parler de, and the de comes back after c'est
:::
